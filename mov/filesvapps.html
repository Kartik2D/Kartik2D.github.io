Files vs Apps

How AI is quietly dismantling the app-centric era of computing, and why that matters for who owns your work.

A brief history of computing interfaces

In the beginning, computing was inherently file-centric. During the 80s and 90s, we interacted directly with files: documents, spreadsheets, and code existed as independent entities that could be accessed with various tools. A text file was a text file. You could open it in anything.

The 2000s saw the rise of application ecosystems, where software increasingly became the gateway to our data. By the 2010s, we entered the walled garden era, where apps not only mediated our relationship with files but often obscured them entirely behind proprietary formats and cloud services.

This evolution traded flexibility for convenience, but at a cost to user autonomy.

The cost of app-centrism

Today, we face significant challenges from this app-centric approach. Vendor lock-in means our creative work becomes hostage to specific companies and their business models. Format obsolescence threatens the longevity of our digital artifacts: what happens to your Notion database if the company disappears? Data portability remains an afterthought for many platforms, while subscription fatigue grows as we pay monthly fees for access to our own work. Taken together, these problems point to a fundamental misalignment between creator needs and business incentives.

Enter AI

This shift reveals a profound truth: users primarily care about transforming their data, not mastering interfaces. Traditional applications bundle interfaces, features, and file formats into inseparable units. AI unbundles these components, allowing each to evolve independently.

The request changes from "I need Photoshop" to "I need to edit this image," focusing on outcomes rather than tools.

Value shifts from UI mastery to clear intent expression, and interfaces become ephemeral while data transformation becomes central to the computing experience.

What this looks like in practice

⬡

Rather than purchasing, installing, and learning Adobe Illustrator, a user might simply ask an AI to:

"Code me a custom SVG editor that precisely fits my needs."

⬡

Instead of subscribing to a data visualization platform and navigating its learning curve:

"Build me a data visualization directly from this dataset."

⬡

Rather than adapting to Notion's specific paradigm:

"Create a custom markdown editor aligned with my exact workflow."

Each case represents a move toward purpose-built tools over general-purpose applications. The app is no longer a product you acquire. It's an output you generate, on demand, fitted to your needs.

We may be returning, in a strange way, to something like the file-centric model, but elevated. Not files opened by apps, but intentions fulfilled by intelligence. The tool becomes invisible. The work remains yours.

What app designers can learn from this

The rise of AI-generated, purpose-built tools is a signal. Users aren't abandoning apps because they dislike software. They're abandoning apps that force them to serve the tool rather than the other way around.

The designers who will thrive in this era are those who internalize a simple inversion: the user's outcome is the product, not the app. That reframe has real consequences. It means prioritizing data portability from day one, as a statement of trust. It means designing for intent, not for feature completeness. It means asking not "what can a user do in this app?" but "what does a user need to accomplish, and how little friction can we put between them and that goal?"

The best apps of the next decade won't be walled gardens. They'll be transparent pipes: powerful, opinionated about workflow, but humble about ownership.

There's also a lesson about format. Proprietary data formats are a liability now, not a moat. When an AI can generate a bespoke tool in minutes, lock-in stops being a retention strategy and starts being a reason to leave. Open formats, composable architectures, and export-first thinking are good product strategy, and good ethics too.

Finally, the shift toward AI-mediated computing should push designers to think harder about what makes a general-purpose app worth using at all. The answer, increasingly, is community, collaboration, and trust: things a generated tool can't replicate. Build around those, and your app becomes something no prompt can replace.
