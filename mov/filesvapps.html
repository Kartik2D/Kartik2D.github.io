Files vs Apps

How AI is quietly dismantling the app-centric era of computing, and why that matters for who owns your work.

A brief history

Through the 80s and 90s, computing was file-centric. Documents, spreadsheets, and code lived as independent files you could open with whatever tool you liked. A text file was a text file. You could open it in anything.

In the 2000s, application ecosystems took over and software became the gateway to our data. By the 2010s, we were in the walled garden era: apps mediated our relationship with files and often obscured them entirely behind proprietary formats and cloud services.

Each step traded away user autonomy for convenience.

The cost of app-centrism

Vendor lock-in holds creative work hostage to specific companies and their business models. Format obsolescence threatens its longevity: what happens to your Notion database if the company disappears? Data portability is an afterthought on most platforms, and subscription fatigue grows as we pay monthly fees for access to our own work. The incentives of the companies that build apps have drifted away from the needs of the people who create inside them.

Enter AI

What users actually want is to transform their data. The interface is just the means. Traditional applications bundle interfaces, features, and file formats into inseparable units; AI unbundles them, allowing each to evolve independently.

The request changes from "I need Photoshop" to "I need to edit this image."

When that happens, the interface stops being something you invest years learning. It becomes disposable. The data underneath is what lasts.

What this looks like in practice

⬡

Instead of buying Adobe Illustrator and learning it, a user might simply ask an AI to:

"Code me a custom SVG editor that precisely fits my needs."

⬡

Instead of subscribing to a data visualization platform:

"Build me a data visualization directly from this dataset."

⬡

Instead of bending your workflow to fit Notion:

"Create a custom markdown editor aligned with my exact workflow."

In each case, a purpose-built tool replaces a general-purpose application. The app becomes an output you generate on demand, fitted to the job at hand.

We may be circling back, in a strange way, to something like the file-centric model. The tool becomes invisible. The work remains yours.

What app designers can learn from this

The rise of AI-generated, purpose-built tools is a signal. Users abandon apps that make them serve the tool.

The designers who do well in the coming years will be the ones who treat the user's outcome as the product. That reframe has real consequences. Data portability becomes a day-one priority and a statement of trust. Feature checklists matter less than the amount of friction sitting between the user and what they came to do.

The best apps of the next decade will be transparent pipes: opinionated about workflow, humble about ownership.

There's also a lesson about format. When an AI can generate a bespoke tool in minutes, lock-in stops working as a retention strategy — it just gives people a reason to leave. The proprietary format has turned from moat into liability. Export-first thinking is good product strategy, and good ethics too.

Finally, AI should push designers to ask what makes a general-purpose app worth using at all. The answer, increasingly, is community and trust — things a generated tool can't replicate. Build around those, and no prompt can replace you.
